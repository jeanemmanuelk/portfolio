About

I trained as an engineer, then as an analyst. Now I'm turning both toward one question: how machine learning can be made trustworthy enough for medicine.

I hold a B.S. in Computer Engineering and an M.S. in Data Analytics & Information Systems from Shepherd University. In Spring 2027 I begin a Ph.D. in Computer Science, where my research will center on artificial intelligence for healthcare.

Research focus

Clinical data is scarce, imbalanced, and high‑stakes. I study how to learn well from it through transfer learning, principled evaluation, and models whose behavior can be explained to the people who rely on them.

CNN vs. transfer learning for butterfly classification

Outcome

Fine‑tuning a pretrained ResNet18 closed most of the gap a from‑scratch network could not: a 29‑point gain over the baseline, and 7 points beyond a frozen feature extractor.

Method

A controlled comparison: the same splits, augmentation, and training budget across all three models, so the only variable was how much prior knowledge the network started with and which layers were allowed to learn. Most of the work was debugging: reading train/validation curves to separate overfitting from underfitting, matching input normalization to the pretrained weights, and tuning learning rates so fine‑tuning adapted the backbone without erasing it.

Why it matters

Fine‑grained, many‑class, few‑examples‑per‑class is the shape of most medical imaging problems. Knowing when to freeze, when to fine‑tune, and how to verify the difference is groundwork for that setting.

02 · Natural language processing

Fake news detection with LSTMs

99%

Test accuracy · TensorFlow / Keras

Outcome

A recurrent classifier that separates real from fabricated articles with 99% accuracy on held‑out data.

Method

An end‑to‑end NLP pipeline (cleaning, normalization, stop‑word removal, tokenization, and sequence padding) feeding a learned embedding layer and an LSTM that reads each article in order rather than as a bag of words.

Why it matters

Near‑ceiling scores invite scrutiny of what a model has actually learned. The same preprocessing and sequence‑modeling discipline carries directly to clinical notes, where text is noisy and errors are costly.

03 · Mathematical oncology · NIH‑funded

Modeling cancer immunotherapy response

3

Peer‑reviewed posters, PWVAS

3 yrs

West Virginia INBRE program

Outcome

Three years (2018–2021) of NIH‑funded research through West Virginia INBRE, advised by Dr. Qing Wang, presented in three peer‑reviewed posters at the Proceedings of the West Virginia Academy of Science.

Method

Impulsive ordinary differential equations model the interaction between tumor and immune cells, with each treatment dose represented as an instantaneous jump in the system. This lets dosing schedules be varied and their long‑run effect on tumor growth studied analytically and numerically.

Why it matters

Mechanistic models explain why a therapy works; learned models predict whether it will. My research aims to bring the two together.
